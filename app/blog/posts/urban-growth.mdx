# Predicting Urban Economic Growth from Neighborhood Structure

Team project for **CIS 5450: Big Data Analytics** at Penn, with Cindy Li, Sarah Ling, and Tianyi Gu.

## Research Question

To what extent can a neighborhood's physical structure — road density, building density, land-use mix, points of interest — explain or predict its economic growth over time?

## What We Built

We built a ~60,000-row tract-year panel dataset (2016–2024) across Philadelphia, Boston, and New York City by linking four independently sourced datasets:

- **U.S. Census TIGER/Line** tract boundaries (spatial base unit, keyed by GEOID)
- **OpenStreetMap** (via the Ohsome Historical API) for roads, buildings, land use, and points of interest
- **NASA VIIRS Black Marble nighttime-light** satellite composites, used as a proxy for economic activity
- **U.S. Census population** data, used as a density control

Linking meant spatially joining raw OSM geometries to census tracts with `geopandas.sjoin`, computing zonal statistics on the VIIRS rasters with `rasterstats`, and merging everything into one panel via a DuckDB SQL join on `(GEOID, year)`.

## Feature Engineering

From the raw geometries we engineered six families of urban-structure features: road density, intersection density, building count/footprint area, land-use mix (Shannon entropy), POI diversity/count, and year-over-year change features including a lagged nighttime-light term. We used entropy rather than raw counts so the features capture *diversity*, not just volume — a tract with 100 restaurants scores low, a tract with a balanced mix of restaurants, shops, parks, and schools scores high.

## Modeling

We compared a linear baseline (OLS / Ridge / Lasso) against nonlinear tree-based models:

The ~7-point R² gain from tree-based models over linear regression confirmed meaningful nonlinearities and feature interactions. Random Forest slightly outperformed XGBoost on test data but showed more train/test overfitting, typical for deep forests.

SHAP analysis on the XGBoost model showed lagged nighttime-light level was the strongest predictor (mean reversion), with building count and land-use/POI entropy contributing smaller, context-dependent effects.

## Feature Selection

We used two complementary approaches to keep the feature space efficient: pairwise correlation dropping (removing any feature pair with |r| > 0.9, fit on training data only to avoid leakage — no pairs exceeded that threshold) and LassoCV regularization, which drove uninformative coefficients toward zero without eliminating most features, confirming the feature set carried real signal.

## Takeaways

- Time and location both matter — the model needed year and metro fixed effects to account for COVID-era volatility and cross-city differences.
- Most individual structural features are weak predictors on their own; the dominant signal comes from the lagged economic baseline (mean reversion), with structure adding smaller marginal effects.
- Results are associative, not causal, and limited to three metros — expanding coverage and adding causal methods (e.g. diff-in-diff) are natural next steps.
